## Course description

An introductory programming course using Python, emphasizing algorithm design, problem-solving strategies, program design, variables, types, expressions, control structures, input/output, functions, strings, collections, classes and objects, modules, files, searching, sorting, and the relationship between recursion and iteration as time permits.

## Course purpose

Build a durable programming foundation while learning to explain, test, revise, collaborate, use source control, and use AI as a tool that must be checked rather than trusted automatically.

## Required materials and resources

- Python 3 in the approved course environment.
- Canvas access and a reliable way to submit code, output, evidence, and explanations.
- No required textbook or external course. CS50P and other open references may be recommended, but students do not need to purchase ZyBooks or another commercial text.
- Week 2 uses the course Local AI Lab path. Final release/download details remain subject to the course repository and Canvas instructions.

Materials used in connection with this course may be subject to copyright protection. See the U.S. Copyright Office at https://www.copyright.gov/.

## Course learning goals

1. Implement a Python program to solve a problem.
2. Evaluate whether a program actually solves the intended problem.
3. Modify a program to solve a similar or extended problem.
4. Explain the problem, approach, code, output, and evidence that a solution works.
5. Use testing, feedback, peer discussion, and revision to improve a program.
6. Use AI and other resources responsibly, verify suggestions, and disclose meaningful assistance.

## Learning activities and assignments

- Semester kickoff and professional-pathway work.
- Weekly Reasoning Odyssey gates that apply the current programming concept inside a student-chosen world.
- Three larger Reasoning Odyssey checkpoints in Weeks 6, 9, and 14.
- Paired-programming reports, Show-and-Tell reflection, and Friday feedback reports.
- Monday Moment, Wacky Wednesday, and Fun Friday learning/reflection touchpoints.
- Final evidence-backed reflection during finals week.

Assignments are evidence-bearing work. Students should show what they tried, what happened, and why the evidence supports or challenges the conclusion. Canvas is authoritative for the exact submission location and due timestamp.

## Attendance and participation

Attendance and participation are part of the course because programming is learned through explanation, paired work, demonstration, and feedback. The cross-listed online section completes equivalent academic participation through the normal course activities. Attendance/participation is 5% of the final grade. The old escalating absence/letter-grade penalty ladder from historical syllabi is not part of the Fall 2026 course.

## Late work, revision, and recovery

The computing curriculum uses a shared continuous late-work default unless a course source explicitly overrides it. Work is graded for academic quality first. Effective lateness then reduces that earned score continuously at 1% per 24 hours late, including fractional days, after any instructor-granted excused late time. Assignments may remain open after the due date so recovery is still worthwhile, subject to the real end-of-semester grading boundary. Revisions/resubmissions may be accepted where the course workflow permits them; course-specific highest-score and drop-lowest rules in the repository control when applicable. Canvas holds the authoritative due/submission timestamps.

## Artificial intelligence and outside resources

AI is welcome as a learning and investigation tool when the assignment permits it. Students may use AI to brainstorm, explain, compare approaches, debug, revise, or propose code. AI is not the authority for the submitted work. Students remain responsible for accuracy, originality, privacy, and academic integrity. When AI materially contributes, disclose the tool and what it did, then name the independent evidence used to verify the result. A typical disclosure is: ‘I used [tool] for [role]. I verified the result with [test, run, source, calculation, comparison, or explanation].’
